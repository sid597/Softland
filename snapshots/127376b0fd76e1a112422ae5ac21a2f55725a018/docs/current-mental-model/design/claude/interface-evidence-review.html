What Kind of Interface Does This World Need?
The Evidence Review

Softland design research · 2026-06-10 · tests the load-bearing claims of the settlement thesis against ~23 fetched sources (20 from the research harness + 3 targeted gap-fills). Verification pass intentionally skipped; confidence flags are editorial judgment of source quality and convergence, stated per finding.

Executive summary

The settlement thesis — persistent rooms over one address space, trail as memory, judgment in context, geography grown not computed, time as the first zoom surface — survives contact with the literature, but not unchanged. Two corrections were forced: candidate hygiene is a volume-and-redundancy problem, not a time-decay problem; and place-ness has a fifth load-bearing ingredient the thesis missed — visible co-presence of other minds. One claim got sharper: inferred spatial layouts are legible (people correctly read proximity as similarity) but not inhabitable (return and revisitation collapse under layout instability — and machine-meaningful regions actually lost to a meaningless-but-stable grid). One genuine discovery: the graduate-on-touch / candidates-until-accepted design in the object-container spec is a direct descendant of a validated 30-year research lineage — Shipman & Marshall's incremental formalization — which supplies both confidence and concrete design rules. The braid slice picked up hard numbers: people compress sessions into ~7 semantic segments, raw exploratory history runs ~300 versions/hour, abandoned branches outnumber revisited ones ~12:1, and the braid's core bet (cross-lane linking) is precisely the operation users find hardest (66% success), so those links must be few, precise, and privileged.

Method & coverage

A deep-research harness decomposed the brief into five angles (Q1 academic record; Q1 contrarian; Q2 trails/replay/provenance; Q3 in-flow judgment; Q4 place-ness), ran five search agents, and fetched ~20 sources with claim extraction before being stopped. Per Sid's call, the 3-vote adversarial verification phase was skipped (75 agent invocations saved); synthesis was done by hand from the extracted claims, with quotes preserved in the appendix. Three gaps were then filled with direct fetches: Shipman & Marshall's Formality Considered Harmful (incremental formalization), Archambault & Purchase's mental-map experiments (abstract + secondary reporting only — full text paywalled), and a 2025 uncertainty-visualization study. Four searches probed Prezi-vs-slides evidence and embedding-map usability.

How to weigh this document: every finding cites its source and the appendix carries the verbatim extracted quotes, but no claim was independently re-verified. Findings marked high rest on peer-reviewed primary sources with statistics or on multiple converging sources; medium on a single primary source or secondary reporting; low on contested, self-report-heavy, or extrapolated evidence.

Q1 — Zoomable & spatial UIs: the actual record

Verdict: NUANCED — the trichotomy survives with an upgrade. "Given / authored / inferred" was the right cut, but the evidence splits inferred into two capabilities the thesis conflated: inferred geometry can be read; it cannot be lived in. The moderating variable is layout stability, and its benefits concentrate exactly on the tasks that make a place a place: orientation, return, revisitation.

The failure mode is real and pervasive

Desert fog is inherent to multiscale worlds, hitting novices and experts alike high
Jul & Furnas documented "desert fog" — views containing no information on which to base a navigation decision — as a structural property of zoomable interfaces, observed in Pad++ with severe navigation problems for both novice and experienced users. This is the canonical ZUI failure mode, not an implementation bug.
Jul & Furnas, UIST '98 — Critical Zones in Desert Fog
Implication: any continuous-zoom surface in Softland needs every view to afford an unambiguous next action (their formal requirement) — which time satisfies trivially (there is always earlier/later) and inferred space does not.
Jul & Furnas tried inferring structure from layout — and abandoned it high
They applied cluster analysis to object positions to derive groupings and found semantics and spatial layout hopelessly confounded: algorithmic groupings "risk falsely conveying meaningful structure the author never intended." They switched to view-based grouping — groups defined by what a view contains rather than by spatial proximity.
Jul & Furnas, UIST '98 (same paper)
Implication: view-based grouping is, structurally, the room. The ZUI pioneers' own retreat lands on the settlement's primitive.
Desert fog appears even on real geographic maps — and embedded multi-level structure beats a separate overview high
In a 32-subject controlled TOCHI study: removing the overview pane did not reduce accuracy, subjects were 22% faster without it on a semantically multi-level map, and actively using the overview cost ~20% (overview↔detail switching correlated with completion time, r = .404). Yet 80% preferred having the overview — a clean satisfaction/performance dissociation. Desert fog occurred for 6/32 subjects on real maps; the multi-level map cut it to 2.
Hornbæk, Bederson & Plaisant, TOCHI 2002 — Navigation patterns and usability of zoomable UIs
Implication: build multiscale cues into the braid itself (level-labeled structure, landmarks in-stream); treat any separate overview/census strip as metadata, not navigation. Expect users to ask for an overview anyway — preference ≠ performance.

Authored geometry: durable, personal, real

User-placed layouts beat hierarchies — and the authors explicitly contrast manual vs automatic placement high
Data Mountain (user-arranged web-page thumbnails on an inclined plane) reliably beat IE4 Favorites on retrieval time (F(2,18)=4.84, p<.02) and failed retrievals (F(2,18)=8.3, p<.01). The design rationale is explicit: manual placement exploits long-term spatial memory; automatic layout serves only short-term use. Layouts were idiosyncratic, personally meaningful, with no "correct" arrangement, evolving under user control.
Robertson, Czerwinski et al., UIST '98 — Data Mountain
Implication: the grown-geography lifecycle (arrangement as the act that creates spatial meaning) has direct experimental support; the system's job is to make placement cheap and then never move things.
Authored spatial memory survives months — on location alone high
Follow-up study: subjects retrieved their 100 arranged pages after several months with no significant slowdown. When thumbnails were blanked to plain icons, retrieval dropped, then recovered to full speed on location memory alone.
Czerwinski, van Dantzich, Robertson & Hoffman, INTERACT '99 — Thumbnail, mouse-over text and spatial location memory
Implication: rooms whose arrangement the inhabitant authored are a months-scale memory asset — the J7 "return" job has a mechanism.
The spatial-memory benefit was contested even inside the Data Mountain paper medium
The authors themselves cite Jones & Dumais (1986): spatial location adds little retrieval value beyond a semantic label. They claim their results show otherwise but acknowledge the open question. And performance preceded preference — the first study group preferred the familiar IE4 despite retrieving faster on Data Mountain; only after interaction polish did 8/11 prefer it.
Implication: don't read early "rooms feel rough" reactions as thesis failure; and don't oversell spatial arrangement as magic — names/labels carry much of the load.

Stability is the variable — and it matters for return, not reading

Revisitation dominates real navigation, and layout instability is what breaks it high
Observing domain experts on force-directed layouts: over 50% of navigation was revisitation of previously visited regions, and experts reported session-to-session layout variation made recall and re-finding harder. Adding a static colored background grid to an algorithmic layout significantly improved revisitation speed and accuracy (F(1,15)=33.35 time, F(1,15)=27.55 errors, both p≤.001); discrete landmarks helped too (F=6.04 time, F=21.42 errors).
Ghani & Elmqvist — Improving Revisitation in Graphs through Static Spatial Features
Implication: the settlement's core bet — that return is the dominant act of inhabitation — matches measured behavior. Stability + landmarks are the enabling conditions.
The killer detail: machine-meaningful regions LOST to a meaningless-but-stable grid high
The same work tested data-derived regionalization (Voronoi regions computed from node clustering) against an arbitrary uniform 3×3 grid. The data-derived version performed significantly worse — participants said the irregular, data-driven boundaries confused rather than helped. The authors' own hypothesis was rejected.
Implication: when Softland proposes spatial scaffolding for the grown geography, propose simple, stable, named structure — not semantically-derived blobs. Meaning comes from the inhabitant; the system supplies stable bones.
Spatial memory survey: stable arrangement is a performance resource; rearrangement destroys it high
The Foundations & Trends survey consolidates the psychology + HCI evidence: strong location knowledge enables fluid interaction without visual search; interfaces that move or rearrange items break it. Crucial scope note: this evidence covers object-location memory (placing/retrieving items in bounded spaces — rooms, canvases), not large-scale ZUI navigation, which is desert-fog territory.
Scarr, Cockburn & Gutwin, 2013 — Supporting and Exploiting Spatial Memory in UIs
Implication: rooms and boards inherit a solid evidence base; a global zoomable landscape does not inherit it automatically.
Mental-map preservation: no conclusive comprehension benefit — but orientation tasks benefit medium
Archambault & Purchase's synthesis of dynamic-graph experiments: "no experiment has found conclusive evidence that supports the effectiveness of the mental map in the comprehension of a dynamic graph series" — yet for orientation tasks, preserving the mental map is significantly faster with fewer errors; the benefit is task-dependent. (Abstract + secondary reporting; full text paywalled.)
Archambault & Purchase, IJHCS 2013 — The "Map" in the mental map
Implication: perfectly consistent with the legible/inhabitable split — stability buys little for one-shot reading and a lot for knowing where you are and returning. Spend stability budget on lived-in views, not on every projection.

Counter-evidence: inferred layouts do work — as instruments

People correctly decode proximity-as-similarity in inferred maps medium
Fabrikant et al. confirmed the "first law of cognitive geography" on point-display spatializations: users read closer points as more semantically similar. Scope caveat flagged in the paper: this tests interpretation, not orientation, navigation, or return. Also: visual channels other than distance (cluster shapes) leak unintended signal.
Fabrikant et al. — Testing the First Law of Cognitive Geography
Search inside inferred 2D spatializations works; higher-fidelity 3D made things worse medium
Westerman & Cribbin: users completed search tasks in machine-inferred 2D document maps; a 3D layout capturing 50–70% more semantic variance produced no performance gain — the added navigational burden ate the fidelity. Performance varied with mapping quality and users' spatial ability (an equity concern: inferred geography won't serve everyone equally).
Westerman & Cribbin, IJHCS 2000 — Mapping semantic information in virtual space
ZUI presentations: engagement up, comprehension contested low
The Harvard Prezi study found audiences rated zoomable presentations more organized, engaging, and persuasive than PowerPoint — but it is self-report-heavy. Learning studies split: one found more conceptual learning and less cognitive load with Prezi; another found PowerPoint better for long-term retention. No clean win either way.
Chou et al., Computers & Education · Harvard study coverage
Implication: zoom-as-rhetoric (presentations, guided descents through the braid) is defensible for engagement; don't claim comprehension gains from zoom itself.
Modern embedding maps: a genuine evidence vacuum — plus a known geometry-reliability problem medium
Searches for usability studies of embedding-based corpus maps (Nomic Atlas, WizMap-class tools) surfaced tool papers and technique comparisons, but no comprehensive study of whether users orient, navigate, and return in them. Meanwhile the dimensionality-reduction literature itself warns that 2D t-SNE/UMAP embeddings do not reliably preserve inter-cluster similarity.
UMAP vs t-SNE initialization study · WizMap · Nomic Atlas whitepaper
Implication: the entire current wave of "map of knowledge" products is running ahead of its evidence. If Softland ships an embedding census view, ship it as a frozen, labeled instrument edition — and know that even its distances partially lie.

Q2 — Trails, replay, and provenance

Verdict: SUPPORTED for fact-finding and process-only insight; the deep-understanding version is honestly untested. The braid is buildable on evidence — with hard numbers for compression, landmarks, and culling — but its core differentiator (cross-lane links) is the operation users measurably struggle with, and "history transfers deep understanding" remains a bet the literature has not settled. The felt test is a real experiment.
Newcomers can answer real questions from someone else's construction history — 80% median success high
Fifteen first-time users navigated a stranger's notebook history of 300+ versions via Verdant's history UI and correctly answered a median 80% of realistic history-foraging questions (which version produced this plot, what parameter changed, etc.). This is the most direct support the braid has: structured navigable history transfers concrete project understanding to people who weren't there.
Kery, John, O'Flaherty, Horvath & Myers, CHI 2019 — Verdant: Effective Foraging in Notebook History
Some understanding exists ONLY in process history high
History Flow on 618,502 Wikipedia revisions surfaced dynamics invisible in any final artifact: first-mover advantage (earliest-contributed text persists longest and is edited least — construction order shapes final content), vandalism repair medians of 1.7–2.8 minutes vs 90.4 minutes for ordinary content. The visualization's impressions were corroborated by independent statistical analysis.
Viégas, Wattenberg & Dave, CHI 2004 — History Flow
Implication: "how did Softland get here" has answer-classes that no curated summary could contain — the braid is not a nice-to-have view of facts available elsewhere.
Raw history is unusable; compression IS the product high
Exploratory work generates ~300 versions per hour of notebook coding. Verdant only became usable through chunking (merging run events within 60s) plus foraging cues — dates, previews, diff highlighting, minimaps. When cues were inadequate, users degraded to brute-force linear replay, lost confidence, and abandoned; 8/15 participants hit "how do I get back?" navigation failures.
Kery et al., CHI 2019 (same study)
Implication: replayable history without scent, filtering, and stable return paths silts up rather than informs. The braid's compression layer is not polish — it's the feature.
Humans compress sessions into ~7 semantic segments, bounded by findings and strategy shifts medium
Asked to compress ~90-minute analysis histories for communication, participants overwhelmingly chose 4–12 coarse segments (mean 8.7, median 7.5, mode 7). Boundaries were driven by semantic events — key data findings (22.9% of coded transitions), approach/strategy changes (27.1%) — not raw interaction events. Insight content rises across the timeline while action content falls, so one static compression rule mismatches how people actually summarize. No consensus granularity exists (3–19 stages for identical recordings) → compression must be user-adjustable. ~66% of what humans put in summaries is machine-capturable from logs; the reasoning layer is not. (Single study, N=18 students, one domain.)
Empirical Insights into Analytic Provenance Summarization (arXiv 2410.11011)
Implication: braid defaults — ~5–9 segments per session; segment on ActionDecisions, doc commits, named findings (the substrate already records these); machine drafts the trail structure, the human supplies the why; granularity slider, not fixed levels.
Cull abandoned branches by default; user landmarks are sacred high
In 20,192 logged Tableau actions, undo outnumbered redo 1,023 to 82 (~12.5×) — abandoned paths are rarely revisited. Behavior-derived chunking rules (formatting runs coalesced — formatting was 23.8% of all actions and 73.6% immediately followed by more formatting; sort/filter chunked under 30s; shelf actions under 5s) culled or coalesced 61.7% of history states. Bookmarking/annotating a state exempted it and its upstream branch from culling. Communication of findings happened via manually curated exported subsets — never raw replay.
Heer, Mackinlay, Stolte & Agrawala, InfoVis 2008 — Graphical Histories for Visualization
Implication: derive chunking rules from observed Softland event patterns (tool-call bursts, edit runs), don't invent them; landmark = exemption from compression; the hand-off artifact is a curated room over the trail, with the trail one gesture beneath.
Temporal spacing mode determines what is visible at all high
History Flow found that date-proportional spacing made rapidly-repaired vandalism literally invisible, while equal-spacing revealed it; and that tight bidirectional linking between history overview and underlying detail (crosshairs, scroll-sync) was necessary, not optional, for analysis to work.
Viégas et al., CHI 2004 (same paper)
Implication: the braid ships with both spacing modes from day one, and braid↔source crosshair linking is part of the minimum slice, not a later polish item.
The braid's core bet is the operation users find hardest high
In Verdant, tasks requiring users to relate versions of two or more separate artifacts had the lowest success rate of all categories — 66%. The braid's whole value proposition is cross-lane relation (transcript ↔ doc ↔ commit).
Implication: cross-lane links must be few, high-precision, visually privileged, and judged in place — and the slice should measure success on cross-lane questions specifically, since that's where it will fail first.
Evidence that provenance tools transfer deep understanding is thin — say so high
A formal review of 50 provenance papers found few convincing evaluations; an independent review found only 2.9% of visualization evaluation papers addressed visual data analysis and reasoning at all. Complete rationale capture — the provenance type a newcomer most needs — is held to be difficult-to-impossible, and continuous rationale elicitation taxes the work itself. Heer et al. never validated transfer experimentally, and semantic zooming of histories was proposed only as untested future work. Annotation effort pays best concentrated at anomalies — divergences from normal progression (Dou et al., via Ragan).
Ragan, Endert, Sanyal & Chen, VAST 2015 — Characterizing Provenance in Visualization
Implication: the theory-of-change framing is correct — the braid is the experiment that tests the under-evidenced claim, on the corpus where you can feel the answer. Their taxonomy (5 provenance types: data, visualization, interaction, insight, rationale; 6 purposes) is a free vocabulary for what braid lanes must distinguish.
The memex-trail lineage: trails work as overlays; the bottleneck is authoring and upkeep medium
Walden's Paths (guided trails over web documents, the direct memex descendant) found in real classroom deployments that the friction was on the author side: paths needed gradual authoring, readers needed a clear visual distinction between source content and the trail author's annotation, paths broke as underlying material changed (link rot), and readers wanted to author their own. The team generalized to "metadocuments" — documents composed of other documents — noting these carry a fundamentally different maintenance and provenance regime.
Shipman, Furuta et al. — Guided Paths through Web-Based Collections
Implication: rooms-over-trails inherit known requirements: author/source visual distinction (calibration, again), graceful behavior when underlying objects change (the spec's drift-surfacing covers this), and authoring that can happen incrementally. Reader-becomes-author is the expected pressure, not an edge case.

Q3 — Judging machine-proposed structure

Verdict: in-flow judgment SUPPORTED; aging/decay REFUTED. The evidence converges from four independent directions — mixed-initiative theory, clinical alert fatigue at scale, AI-overreliance experiments, and Copilot telemetry — on the same shape: surface fewer, better candidates in context; never repeat a rejected one; place friction only at consequential boundaries; and do not trust acceptance as a quality signal.
Mixed-initiative theory gives the firing rule: act / ask-in-context / stay silent high
Horvitz's principles formalize when machine initiative should fire: act autonomously only above a context-dependent expected-utility threshold; below a second threshold, do nothing; between them, engage in lightweight in-context dialog — because asking costs less than wrongly acting but more than silence. Under uncertainty, degrade gracefully: propose a coarser scope rather than a specific wrong guess (LookOut falls back from exact times to day/week views the user refines). Wrong guesses must be cheap to dismiss with natural gestures, leaving the user in an approximately-correct position. Even timing is measurable: dwell-before-accept follows a sigmoid in message size — the right moment to surface a proposal is a function of the artifact being read.
Horvitz, CHI '99 — Principles of Mixed-Initiative User Interfaces
Implication: Softland's candidate pipeline gets three confidence bands, not two: high+cheap-to-undo → auto-apply with a visible event; intermediate → in-flow candidate; low → withheld entirely. Situators should emit coarse candidates when unsure ("these two sources are related somehow") rather than precise wrong ones ("this supports that").
The decay correction: fatigue is volume + redundancy; time-based desensitization doesn't exist high
Large-scale clinical decision-support data: each additional best-practice alert per encounter dropped acceptance ~30% (IRR 0.70, p<.001); each 5% increase in the proportion of repeated alerts lowered acceptance 10%; once a clinician overrode an alert, repeats were overridden 87.9% (advisories) to 99.9% (drug alerts) of the time. Crucially: no evidence of time-based desensitization — newly deployed alerts did not decay in response rate over time — and raw user workload didn't predict acceptance; alert volume and informativeness did. The authors' primary recommendation: cut redundant proposals, don't re-present them.
Ancker et al., BMC Med Inform Decis Mak 2017
Implication: replace the derivation doc's "candidate aging/decay" with: per-view candidate caps; a judged-rejected candidate never reappears unchanged (only with new evidence, visibly marked as such); low-value candidates suppressed at the source. The silting threat is real — the timer is the wrong tool.
Over-acceptance is the deep risk: explanations don't help, heuristics replace judgment, friction works but hurts high
Buçinca et al. (N=199): adding explanations to AI suggestions does not reduce overreliance and can increase it; people form global accept-the-AI heuristics instead of evaluating each item. Cognitive forcing functions (delaying or withholding the AI answer) significantly reduced overreliance — and received the worst subjective ratings, benefiting high-Need-for-Cognition users most (an intervention-generated inequality).
To Trust or to Think — Buçinca, Malaya & Gajos, CSCW 2021
Implication: per-candidate judgment stays one cheap gesture (anything heavier gets heuristic acceptance anyway); deliberate friction is reserved for consequential boundaries — graduation to durable, acceptance into truth, promotion to public-material. This independently confirms the derivation's "select must not graduate" correction: cheap gestures collect heuristic, not deliberate, judgment.
Withholding works at deployment scale — and acceptance rate is a flawed quality signal high
On telemetry from 535 Copilot programmers, a conditional-display policy (CDHF) could withhold a significant fraction of suggestions that would have been rejected, cutting verification burden; modeling the programmer's latent state (what they're doing) mattered for timing. Key negative result: using human acceptance as the reward signal for what to display degrades suggestion quality — acceptance is a proxy, not a measure.
Mozannar et al., 2023 — When to Show a Suggestion? · context: Ziegler et al., CACM
Implication: never tune situators on acceptance rates; track downstream survival instead (did the accepted relation get used, cited, built upon — the substrate's provenance graph can measure this directly).
Calibration display has measurable effects — strongest on skeptics medium
A 2025 study (N=147): visualizing AI uncertainty changed ~33% of participants' decisions; trust was significantly reinforced specifically among participants with negative prior attitudes toward AI (p<.01). Size was perceived as carrying the most information; color saturation was most intuitive; the authors decline to endorse one channel universally.
Frontiers in Computer Science, 2025
Implication: the epistemic framework's calibration priority (fact / hypothesis / inference / guess, visibly distinct) is not just honesty — it's a trust mechanism that works hardest for skeptical visitors, exactly the audience public-form Softland must win. Channel choice (size vs saturation) is an empirical question for later.

Q4 — Place-ness without geometry

Verdict: SUPPORTED, about as strongly as a theory claim can be — with one amendment. Space is the opportunity; place is the understood reality. Geometry is neither necessary (text MUDs, USENET) nor sufficient (inert MUD geography, failed CVEs). But the presence studies say the thesis formula was missing its fifth element: other minds, visibly present. Marks matter because someone left them and someone will find them.
The canonical frame: place is appropriated, not designed high (theory + cases)
Harrison & Dourish: the behavioral framing designers seek from spatial models is not a property of space at all — it comes from mutually-held cultural understandings. "Space is the opportunity; place is the understood reality." Place-ness cannot be designed in or computed; it emerges from patterns of use, adaptation, and appropriation — designers can only design for place. Ten years later Dourish went further: place comes first in experience; space/geometry is a derived abstraction; working spatial structure is produced from within by collective use, and top-down spatial imposition fails. As a historical outcome: geometry-first Collaborative Virtual Environments did not become how people collaborate remotely.
Harrison & Dourish, CSCW '96 · Dourish, CSCW 2006 — Re-Space-ing Place
Implication: double-edged for the settlement: it validates grown-over-computed geography completely — and it humbles the room design too. Even authored structure only becomes place through inhabitation. Ship conditions for appropriation, not finished places.
Text-only environments are robustly experienced as places high
LambdaMOO — purely textual, geometry-free — was experienced by users as a place they visited and were situated within; ordinary social mechanisms of physical places (gathering, norms) operated fully; users persistently extended the world, adding rooms, exits, and notes others later encountered (persistence of marks, working). Its spatial structure was purely topological: discrete rooms, named exits, room-scoped visibility — stable addresses plus dense onward paths sufficed for navigable structure. USENET groups developed distinct place-norms on identical technology — place distinguished purely by social convention.
Curtis — Mudding (LambdaMOO) · Harrison & Dourish (above)
The amendment: presence tracked people, not space — 0 of 207 credited the spatial metaphor high
Across six user groups (N=207), 69% reported a sense of presence in text-only MUDs — and not one subject spontaneously credited the room descriptions or geography. Presence varied with social co-presence (who you were with) and community context (88% in structured lab groups, 74% at a conference, 60% among random LambdaMOO users). Scope note: the instrument measured social presence specifically.
Towell & Towell — Presence in MUDs
Implication: the settlement formula becomes addresses + persistence of marks + paths + memory + others' visible presence. For solo-Softland the co-present others are the agents — Claude and Codex sessions are other minds leaving trails. Their marks should render as inhabitants' traces (who was here, what they touched, what they left), not as system output. The braid's lanes already are this; name it and design it as presence.
Grown geography has a working 1998 precedent — and a warning about rendering wear medium
The Juggler MOO implemented read-wear geography: exits accumulated usage counts and well-trodden paths surfaced in room descriptions — paths literally became roads. The authors argue an information city "cannot be laid out in one step but must develop over time out of inhabitants' interactions," seeded from minimal landmark kernels. Two sharp details: the same wear data was understood by everyone as text but confused users as graphics; and metaphor-breaking shortcuts (teleports, search) were necessary at scale but had to stay sparse and clearly marked, or spatial structure collapsed into unstructured hypertext. Supporting context: 58% of an individual's web accesses are revisits (Tauscher & Greenberg, cited therein).
Dieberger & Frank — A City Metaphor for Information Spaces
Implication: ship read-wear as marks and words before glows and heatmaps — a direct check on WebGPU-spectacle instincts. Summon/search are the settlement's teleports: keep them, mark them as crossings, don't let them replace paths entirely.
Appropriability beats fidelity medium
In paired media-space deployments, Xerox's cheap, movable, user-adjustable video link fostered place and succeeded; Bellcore's high-fidelity but un-appropriable VideoWindow failed — users couldn't make it theirs.
Harrison & Dourish, CSCW '96 (above)
Implication: rooms must be cheaply rearrangeable, markable, and renameable from day one. Rough-but-mine beats polished-but-fixed. (With Data Mountain's caveat: expect preference to lag performance until polish lands.)
Weak spot, flagged: virtual place attachment is extrapolated, not measured low
The virtual-worlds place-attachment literature (Second Life era) argues attachment transfers to virtual places, but as of its writing the place-attachment tradition had measured only physical settings — theoretical extrapolation, not replicated findings.
On Place Attachments in Virtual Worlds

Q5 (unplanned) — Incremental formalization: the graduation precedent

Verdict: the object-container spec's candidates / DerivedUnit / graduate-on-touch design is a direct descendant of a validated research lineage — Shipman & Marshall's incremental formalization — complete with thirty years of documented failures of the alternative (premature formality). This wasn't in the research brief; it surfaced in gap-filling and is the strongest single endorsement the substrate design received.
Premature formality fails for documented, recurring reasons high
Across NoteCards, Aquanet, Trellis, and office studies: users couldn't chunk ("How big is an idea?"), typed links were "rarely used, and when they were, seldom used consistently"; even knowledge-representation experts "had problems formalizing previously implicit structures"; design students who naturally followed IBIS argumentation structure could not produce it when asked to formalize; and Malone's office workers left papers unfiled because filing forces a premature decision — "Leaving them out means that I defer for now having to decide… where to put them."
Shipman & Marshall — Formality Considered Harmful (CSCW journal, 1999)
Implication: every Softland temptation to ask the user to type a relation, name a unit, or classify a candidate up-front is this failure mode wearing a new coat. The substrate already encodes the right answer; hold the line in the interface.
The validated alternative is exactly the spec's lifecycle high
Incremental formalization: informal entry first; the system infers patterns from textual/spatial information and proposes formalization (VIKI's heuristic recognition of visual/spatial patterns = Situate); users confirm or reject suggestions (= judgment); formalisms stay tailorable; structure is added "less at a time" later in the task, when it has become useful (= graduate on touch). The Virtual Notebook System showed adoption grows through use: heavy users accreted reusable structured templates — rituals crystallizing into structure, the same developmental law as "commands are larval workflows."
Shipman & Marshall (above) · Marshall & Shipman — Spatial Hypertext: Designing for Change, CACM
Implication: three design rules imported from the lineage — a suggested structure must be confirmable in one gesture; deferrable forever without penalty; and tailorable after acceptance. Ghani & Elmqvist — Improving Revisitation in Graphs through Static Spatial Features
primary · Q1 · Q1 contrarian: do inferred layouts ever work? · 2011 (Graphics Interface 2011; year inferred from venue record and references dated through December 2010 — the PDF body does not print a date)
  • CDomain experts (social scientists) using force-directed/spring-embedded graph layouts in NetDraw reported that small layout variations between sessions made recall and revisitation harder, and observation showed more than 50% of their navigation in node-link diagrams was revisitation of previously visited regions — establishing both that revisitation is the dominant navigation task and that unstable inferred layouts impede it.
    quote
    Even though both scientists thought that they generally remembered the overall structure of a known graph, they conceded that the spring-embedding algorithm in NetDraw causes slight variations in graph layout that make recall and revisitation more difficult. Moreover, ad hoc observations of social scientists performing SNA showed that more than 50% of all navigations in a node-link diagram was between previously visited parts of a graph.
  • CAdding a static colored background grid (substrate encoding) to an algorithmically generated (lin-log) graph layout significantly improved revisitation speed and accuracy versus a plain graph (Study 3: RM-ANOVA F(1,15)=33.35 for time, F(1,15)=27.55 for ID errors, both p<=0.001) — counter-evidence to the strong claim that inferred layouts cannot support orientation and return: they can, when overlaid with stable authored spatial reference features.
    quote
    Techniques with substrate encoding were significantly faster, and were not less accurate (partially confirming H4)

    Space-driven partitioning using a grid yields significantly faster and more accurate performance than detail-driven partitioning using a Voronoi diagram (rejecting H1)

    quote
    Participants also stated that the combination of substrate encoding and landmarks was the optimal technique because it allowed them to remember the nodes on two levels: globally by background color, and locally by landmarks and boundaries.
  • SThe entire static-spatial-features approach is conditional on the layout not changing: the authors explicitly state that a dynamic layout (e.g., live force-directed) invalidates the user's conceptual map, which directly supports the mental-map-preservation / layout-stability requirement in the Softland thesis.

    We should note that our static approach to spatial graph features relies on the graph layout being static, and that a dynamic layout (such as a force-directed one) may cause the user's conceptual map of the graph to become inaccurate.

    quote
    In this paper, we introduce the problem of "desert fog," a condition wherein a view of an information world contains no information on which to base navigational decisions. ... Desert fog can occur in many electronic environments, but is inherent and pervasive in multiscale worlds. ... In our experience with Pad++ [1], a widely-distributed experimental multiscale environment, desert fog causes severe navigational problems for both novice and experienced users. With infinite scale, there is always more room between things to explore.
  • CInferring structure from spatial layout fails: the authors applied cluster analysis to object layout and found that semantics and spatial layout are confounded, and that algorithmically derived groupings risk falsely conveying meaningful structure the author never intended — leading them to abandon layout-based grouping for view-based grouping.     quote
    Semantics and spatial layout are confounded making it necessary to have some understanding of the semantics of the objects in order to provide meaningful navigational guidance. A more insidious problem is the danger of falsely conveying an impression of meaningful structure. The hierarchical structure identified by cluster analysis may or may not have been intended—and therefore be meaningful—by the author(s) of the world, but users may be inclined to assume that it was.     quote
    The analysis revealed two properties that must hold for a general solution to desert fog. First, there must be a single unambiguous action to be taken in a desert fog view. ... Second, all navigational information provided must be structured so that only a small amount is presented in any given view.

    quote
    Although our experience with tracking a single critical zone is limited, we have found it to be surprisingly helpful. Merely distinguishing desert fog views from interesting views is helpful (and anxiety-reducing). ... We are still seeking to understand the practical implications of view-based navigational aids, in general, and of critical zones, in particular. We are planning user studies to test the sufficiency of critical zones for navigational purposes.

    Our present work has concentrated on automatically showing where there are things of interest. The next major step of research is to show what those things are. Harrison & Dourish (CSCW'96) — Re-Place-ing Space: The Roles of Place and Space in Collaborative Systems
primary · Q4 · Q4 place-ness without geometry · 1996-11-16
  • CThe behavioral framing that designers seek from spatial models is not a property of space at all; it is rooted in mutually-held cultural understandings — i.e., place, not space, frames appropriate behavior. The paper's core principle: space is the opportunity, place is the understood reality.
    quote
    the critical property which designers are seeking, which we call appropriate behavioural framing, is not rooted in the properties of space at all. Instead, it is rooted in sets of mutually-held, and mutually available, cultural understandings about behaviour and action. In contrast to "space", we call this a sense of "place". Our principle is: "Space is the opportunity; place is the understood reality".
  • CPlace-ness cannot be designed in or computed; it emerges only from users' patterns of use, adaptation, and appropriation over time. Designers can only design FOR place by putting tools in occupants' hands. This directly supports Softland's 'geography grown by use rather than computed' thesis — and constrains it: even authored/system-provided structure only becomes place through inhabitation.
    quote
    Placeness is created and sustained by patterns of use; it's not something we can design in. ... It is only over time, and with active participation and appropriation, that a sense of place begins to permeate these systems. The sense of place must be forged by the users; it cannot be inherent in the system itself.
  • CGeometry is not necessary for place-ness: text-only, non-spatial environments (USENET groups, mailing lists, social navigation via hotlists/recommenders) exhibit genuine place-ness — distinct norms, styles, and behavioral framing — without any underlying spatial model. Identical technology hosts different places distinguished purely by social convention.
    quote
    The technology of each USENET group is exactly the same, and yet the resultant groups exhibit very different notions of place. ... This placeness builds upon the tension between connectedness and distinction which we raised earlier; but, critically, it emerges without an underlying notion of space.
  • CGeometry is not sufficient and can be inert: in MUDs the spatial metaphor (rooms, geography, connectedness) contributes far less to managing interaction than designers assume — teleporting and inter-room messaging undermine it, newbies' norm violations persist despite legible geography — and what actually does the work is the socially-established places. Embedding placeness in spatial metaphors can even undermine appropriation (the 'virtual door' paradox).
    quote
    The spatial metaphor is actually of much less value in controlling interaction, engagement and so forth than might be imagined. Where present, common MUD facilities like teleporting or inter-room messaging undermine it even more. The spatial metaphor—the connectedness of space, and the geography of the MUD—breaks down, and only the places remain.
  • SAppropriability beats fidelity: in paired media-space experiments, Xerox's cheap, movable, user-adjustable video link fostered a sense of place and succeeded, while Bellcore's high-fidelity but un-appropriable VideoWindow system failed — users could not make it theirs. Design implication for Softland's rooms: persistent projections must be cheaply rearrangeable by inhabitants (marks, furniture-moving) or place-ness will not form regardless of rendering quality.
    quote
    the forbidding equipment, complex and delicate to configure, could not be appropriated by its users. It wasn't theirs, and they could not make it theirs. This separation between users and technology could be seen to inhibit the community's adoption of the technology.

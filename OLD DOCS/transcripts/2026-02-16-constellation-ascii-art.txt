  ★ Insight ─────────────────────────────────────
  This brief is essentially asking: what if code review wasn't reading a 
  document, but navigating a knowledge graph? The key tension is between the
  linearity of reading (how humans consume) and the graph structure of reasoning
   (how decisions actually form). The wildest UI would make the graph feel
  natural to traverse — like walking through a building, not parsing a tree.
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────

  Here's my vision — a Reasoning Constellation on an infinite WebGPU canvas with
   semantic zoom. I'll show it at three zoom levels, because progressive
  disclosure IS the UI:

    CMD+K ──────────── Fuzzy search across all claims/evidence/decisions
    ESC ────────────── Zoom out one level (portal → thread → orbit)
    TAB ────────────── Next step in guided traversal

  ★ Insight ─────────────────────────────────────
  The common thread across all 10 visions:

  1. Every design makes GAPS visible — whether it's hollow rock, dark neurons,
  cold cases, dead branches, or NO-GO subsystems. The most powerful feature
  isn't showing what's there — it's making what's MISSING impossible to ignore.
  2. Every design has a "verdict gate" — you can't approve until conditions are
  met. The Crime Board needs all leads resolved. The Metro needs all stations
  clear. Mission Control needs all subsystems GO. This turns review from "read
  and click approve" into "achieve confidence."
  3. Metaphor choice changes behavior — the Newspaper encourages speed-reading.
  The Courtroom encourages adversarial rigor. The Tree encourages nurturing. The
   Metro encourages systematic traversal. The right metaphor for your team IS
  the product decision.
  4. All of these are buildable on your stack — WebGPU gives you the canvas.
  Rama gives you the event log. Missionary gives you the reactivity. The only
  question is: which metaphor best serves the cognitive flow of a developer
  doing review?

  ★ Insight ─────────────────────────────────────
  Discourse graphs (from Joekel, Chan, etc.) represent knowledge as typed nodes
  + typed edges:
  - Node types: Question, Claim, Evidence, Decision, Risk (and we'll add
  Feedback)
  - Edge types: supports, opposes, addresses, informs, supersedes, depends_on
  - The power is that every relationship is explicit and typed — no ambiguity
  about WHY two things are connected. This is what makes them computable
  (confidence propagation, gap detection, staleness tracking).

  This is the architectural breakthrough: all 10 visions from earlier are just
  rendering skins over one universal discourse graph. The data model is:
  1. Nodes with types (Q, C, E, D, R, F) stored as Rama PState entities
  2. Edges with types (supports, opposes, addresses, informs, depends_on,
  supersedes) stored as Rama PState relationships
  3. Confidence propagation is a topology computation over this graph — when an
  opposes edge is added, it propagates upstream through supports and addresses
  edges

  ★ Insight ─────────────────────────────────────
  Notice what the discourse graph tags actually DO to these layouts:

  1. The [Q] tag at the top makes every section feel purposeful — "this section
  exists to answer THIS question." The newspaper goes from "articles" to
  "investigations with conclusions."
  2. The ── supports/opposes ──▶ edge annotations turn passive reading into
  active verification. When you see Ex.B ──supports──▶ C1, C3, C4, you instantly
   know which claims fall apart if that evidence is wrong. In the Courtroom,
  this is literally how a judge thinks: "if I throw out Exhibit B, which counts
  collapse?"
  3. The blocked_by edges on the Verdict are the killer feature. Both UIs now
  have a hard gate — you can SEE exactly what prevents shipping. It's not "I
  feel uneasy" — it's "R1 and R2 are opposes-edges on C3 and C5, and those
  blocks propagate to the Verdict node." Computable, not vibes.

  The metaphor is the skin. The discourse graph is the skeleton. You feel the
  courtroom drama or newspaper urgency, but underneath, every element has a type
   and every connection has a name.

  The Python-verified approach was key here. ASCII art alignment is deceptively
  hard because:

  line up
  2. Unicode box-drawing chars (║, ═, ┌, ─) are each 1 codepoint but some
  terminals render them wider
  3. The risk boxes needed fixed-width box_line() / box_top() helpers — each
  producing exactly 48 chars — rather than freeform text with manually-counted
  padding

   in my editor" and "looks right everywhere."

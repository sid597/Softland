## Turn 2 — Claude

You've walked into the harder half of last turn's question. Last turn was: the body must become material to be grippable. This turn you've found the converse that makes it a real problem: not everything *can* cross into material — something has to stay code, or the material means nothing. And your closing sentence is the whole question stated clean: what must exist as committed code, maintained by you, versus what can live on the ECS/Smalltalk-image layer. Notice what "maintained by me" makes this: every committed line is a liability you hold personally, with your hours; every fact the system holds is an asset it maintains itself. So the boundary you're drawing is the central resource question of the project, not a tidying exercise.

Let me derive the law rather than adjudicate instances, because I think there is one.

Data does nothing. Every kind of data needs something beneath it that reads it and acts — and that something is code, or is data interpreted by other code, grounding out in code eventually. So: **a thing can live as data exactly when its meaning is already enactable by code that exists.** Which gives the two strata their real jobs: code grows the *language* — new kinds of meaning the system can enact; data grows the *world* — new arrangements of meanings it already has. Every time you commit code you're extending the language. Every time something lands as facts, the world got bigger without the language changing. The kernel isn't where features live; it's where *interpreters* live.

Three forces pin a thing to the code side, and they're the tests you can apply to any candidate:

First, *enactment*: it's what makes some kind of fact visible or effective at all — remove it and those facts go dark. Second, *floor*: it must keep working when the data stratum is arbitrarily broken, because it's what you'll see and touch the breakage *with*. The tools that repair X cannot live inside X — this is the oldest lesson of image-based systems, which could corrupt themselves into unopenable. Third, *physics*: transforms bound to the frame loop and the GPU, where data-stratum indirection costs real milliseconds. There's a fourth, quieter one — *trust*: invariants the inside must not be able to edit away. You've already built that one in miniature: the facet-master pattern is exactly this treaty — code holds the floor form, the grammar, the validators; data holds the revisions and the wear. The stratum law already exists in your repo at the scale of one wrap-column policy.

Now text, your instance. Your instinct is right, and the reason matters more than the verdict: the shaper is forced into code by all three tests at once. It's the enactor of text-facts — without it they're invisible. It's in the repair floor — legibility is precisely what you need when everything else is on fire. And it's physics. But notice the boundary runs *through* what you called text rendering, and you drew it correctly when you described the package: string in, positioned coordinates out, shown by Chrome or WebGPU, don't care. The transform is code; everything it eats (the string, the wrap policy) and everything it yields (the coordinates) is material. The "don't care" at both ends is what a clean stratum cut sounds like.

The editor — your test case — dissolves under the same law, and I think that's the actual answer to "can it be an ECS thing, and if not why not." The editor is not a thing, so it doesn't have a stratum. Take it apart: the cursor is an entity — a fact pointing into a fact. Selection, same. The keymap — which key means which operation, in which context — is data, the classic image-resident thing. The behaviors — wrap, indent — are facets, and one of them already is. The view is a face like any other face. What's left, irreducibly, is a small closed set of *primitive edit verbs* — insert, delete, split, join — transforms on facts, kernel-sized, maybe a dozen — plus the commit artery to Rama. So the answer to "should the current editor exist in this form" falls out: it's a pre-material monolith. It was built before the material existed — you said so — which means it *couldn't* be made of the material: view, cursor, keymap, policy, commit all fused into committed code, none of it visible from inside. It doesn't need porting to the new renderer. It needs melting along the stratum line — verbs sink into the kernel, everything else rises into facts. For what it's worth, this is the exact line two self-hosting giants converged on independently: Smalltalk and Emacs both keep redisplay-and-shaping plus primitive buffer operations in a tiny core, and the entire editor-*behavior* lives in the image. Your instinct reproduced their line without the history.

Your one-atom Electric complaint is the same disease, and I'll mark this as derived, not read — I don't know what electric-native says, and I understand what the fence is for: I'm more useful as a clean re-derivation you can diff your own docs against than as an echo of them. The one-atom is transport whose grain is "the whole world as one value." That's the monolithic editor's disease wearing transport clothes: the layer isn't dealing in the material's grain, so nothing is addressable, subscribable, or incremental per-thing. The melt goes the same direction — the artery should carry fact-level changes, the transport equivalent of insert/delete rather than replace-everything. If electric-native says something like that, we've converged from independent directions, which is evidence. If it says something else, one of us is wrong in a way worth finding.

Your 2D/3D question, then, answers itself by generalization: the shaper is not a special fact about text. For every kind of fact the world can hold, the kernel owes exactly one enactor of the same shape — declarative fact in, drawable geometry out, display-agnostic. Text-run → shaper → positioned glyphs. Stroke or shape → tessellator → triangles. Mesh → placer → draw calls. And once you see that, the "three rendering engines" — tldraw-ish freehand, Figma-ish components, 3D — dissolve the way the editor did. There aren't three engines. There's one kernel — the enactor set plus a compositor — and three *dialects of material* above it: freehand is stroke-facts plus an append-point verb, component-land is shape-facts plus constraint-facts, the 3D world is mesh-and-place facts. Assemblies, not engines.

Zooming all the way out, as you asked: the motion you're in is a census — the same one you just ran on dead files, one level up. Everything in the repo walks past the kernel line and sorts three ways: kernel (your liability — small, closed, chosen), material-and-assemblies (the system's holdings — open, visible, editable from inside), and pre-material monoliths — things built before the material existed, which are owed a melt, not maintenance. The triad is kernel. The facet masters are the treaty working. The block editor and the one-atom are monoliths in the melt queue.

Two more layers down, and then I'm at my edge. The law as stated covers nouns; verbs split the same way — a closed set of primitive verbs in the kernel, and *composed* verbs as data, because a composition of primitives is itself a fact: inspectable, editable, shareable. That split is what would let agents grow new behavior without adding to your maintenance. (One honest sighting: a `verb_registry` exists in shared/ — I know only the filename — and it smells like this pressure already pushing on the code.) And because this is Clojure, the strata aren't sealed forever: code is data here, and eval is the one door through which an image-thing can someday become an interpreter-thing without your hands. That door is the far factory rung from last turn — shut today, but it's why the kernel can *shrink* over time instead of only being chosen once.

Where my thinking actually runs out is the waist. The law tells you what kind of things go in the kernel; it doesn't tell you how *high* the kernel should reach. Too low — raw triangles, raw bytes — and everything above is possible but nothing is convenient; you've built assembly language. Too high — block, card, editor as kernel kinds — and the monolith is reborn with better naming. I can defend a first guess at the waist: fact-kinds of text-run, stroke, shape, image, mesh, connector; verbs of insert, delete, move, connect, group. I don't trust it. What I do trust is the measurement that replaces trusting anyone's guess: the kernel is right as long as each new capability keeps turning out to be an assembly of existing kinds and verbs — and it is wrong, specifically and locally wrong, every single time building something forces you to open the IDE. Each IDE-opening names the missing kernel piece. The boundary isn't drawn once; it's measured by where your hands keep being needed. Choosing the waist's actual members against what the repo already grew — that's the walk I can't do alone from here, and it's the next layer down.

Both threads land on my last paragraph, and they do different things to it: the component-library thread gives the waist question its missing base, and the IDE fact breaks my instrument outright — then your fence spitball rebuilds it better than I had it. I'll take them in order, because by the end they turn out to be one design.

Start with what component libraries actually teach, because I don't think it's "components are the right level." Every component library that works is standing on an escape hatch. React's Button bottoms out in DOM nodes; a Figma component opens into plain vectors; the editor-as-card-with-text-area you described decomposes, in any of these systems, back into a lower vocabulary that stays reachable. The libraries that feel "set" — your word, and the right one — are exactly the ones where that seam is sealed: native platform widgets, closed design systems, anywhere you can't open the Button and find shapes. So the lesson is that the waist was never one layer. It's three things: a **floor** (the lowest vocabulary), a **composition law** (how things nest, how layout resolves, how input finds behavior), and an **open seam** between strata. Your either-or — too-low-you-have-nothing versus component-but-it's-set — dissolves, because those two poles aren't rival choices for one layer; they're different strata of one system, and the systems that feel both convenient and unbounded have both, seam open.

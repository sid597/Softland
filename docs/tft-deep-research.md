# The architects of spatial thought

**The field of "tools for thought" and spatial/visual knowledge mapping spans 80 years, hundreds of practitioners, and one persistent conviction: that better external representations of ideas can fundamentally amplify human intelligence.** From Vannevar Bush's 1945 vision of the Memex to Bret Victor's Dynamicland and today's AI-augmented infinite canvases, this lineage connects computer scientists, cognitive researchers, game designers, data artists, and indie tool builders in a remarkably tight intellectual network. What follows is a comprehensive map of the people, labs, projects, and ideas that define this space — organized by era and domain, with the threads between them made visible.

---

## The original visionaries built the conceptual bedrock (1945–1990)

The entire field traces to a handful of papers, demos, and prototypes that established the idea of computers as thinking partners rather than calculating machines.

**Vannevar Bush** (1890–1974) never built his invention, but his 1945 essay "As We May Think" described the Memex — a desk-sized device storing an individual's entire library with **associative trails** linking documents the way the mind links ideas. Bush's key insight was that human thought operates by association, not hierarchical indexing. Ted Nelson reprinted the entire essay in *Literary Machines*. Doug Engelbart read it shortly after publication and it became his life's mission.

**J.C.R. Licklider** (1915–1990) translated Bush's vision into institutional reality. His 1960 paper "Man-Computer Symbiosis" declared that "human brains and computing machines will be coupled together very tightly, and the resulting partnership will think as no human brain has ever thought." As ARPA/IPTO director (1962–1964), Licklider funded Engelbart at SRI, Project MAC at MIT, and the research communities that produced time-sharing, interactive computing, and ultimately the internet. He didn't build the future — he built the ecosystem that built the future.

**Douglas Engelbart** (1925–2013) then demonstrated what that future could look like. His December 9, 1968 presentation — "The Mother of All Demos" — showed the mouse, hypertext linking, real-time collaborative editing, video teleconferencing, and windowed interfaces, all running on the oN-Line System (NLS). His 1962 paper "Augmenting Human Intellect: A Conceptual Framework" remains the philosophical foundation for the entire tools-for-thought movement. Alan Kay, Andy van Dam, and many future pioneers were in the audience.

**Ivan Sutherland** (b. 1938) created the first graphical user interface with Sketchpad (1963), his MIT PhD thesis. Users drew directly on a CRT display with a light pen, manipulated geometric objects, and applied constraints — the ancestor of every CAD program, graphics editor, and spatial computing tool. Sutherland then succeeded Licklider as ARPA/IPTO director (1964–66), connecting the spatial/visual computing tradition directly to federal funding.

**Ted Nelson** (b. 1937) coined "hypertext," "hypermedia," and "hyperlink" in a 1965 ACM paper and launched Project Xanadu in 1960 — an ambitious global hypertext system featuring two-way links, transclusion (reusing content with attribution), and micropayments. With **Andries van Dam** at Brown University, he created the Hypertext Editing System (1967), one of the first two interactive hypertext systems alongside Engelbart's NLS. Nelson has been vocal that Tim Berners-Lee's web implemented only a degraded version of his vision — one-way links, no transclusion, no version tracking. His ZigZag data structure explored multidimensional information organization from the 1990s onward.

**Alan Kay** (b. 1940) synthesized these threads at Xerox PARC. His Dynabook concept (1968–1972) — a tablet-sized personal computer for children — was the blueprint for modern computing. His team created Smalltalk, object-oriented programming, overlapping windows, and the GUI that inspired the Macintosh. Kay's framing of the computer as a **"metamedium"** capable of incorporating all other media drew on Marshall McLuhan, Jerome Bruner, and Seymour Papert. His 2003 Turing Award talk, "The Computer Revolution Hasn't Happened Yet," argued that computing's real potential as a thinking medium remained unrealized — a claim that still motivates the field today.

**Seymour Papert** (1928–2016) contributed the educational philosophy. His Logo programming language featured the Turtle — a creature children could command to draw, making programming tangible, spatial, and embodied. His book *Mindstorms* (1980) articulated constructionism: learning happens best when learners build external artifacts — **"objects to think with."** Papert studied with Piaget in Geneva, co-founded the MIT AI Lab with Marvin Minsky, and directly influenced Kay's Dynabook, Mitchel Resnick's Scratch, and the LEGO Mindstorms kit (named after his book).

**Bill Atkinson** (1951–2025) brought these ideas to millions with HyperCard (1987) — a "software erector set" letting non-programmers build interactive applications by linking cards with text, images, and scripts. HyperCard's principle that **the tool and the output should be the same thing** anticipated modern no-code tools and wikis. Atkinson also invented the menu bar, the selection lasso, MacPaint, and the QuickDraw graphics engine. Apple killed HyperCard in 2004 after years of neglect; Atkinson himself reflected that it should have been networked from the start — in which case it might have become the web.

The genealogy of influence forms a remarkably tight network: Bush → Licklider → Engelbart → Kay → the Macintosh. Bush → Nelson → van Dam → the web. Piaget → Papert → Kay → Smalltalk. Sutherland bridged spatial computing to ARPA funding. These connections aren't incidental — they show how a small community, circulating through a few institutions, built the conceptual foundations for everything that followed.

## Bret Victor turned principles into a movement

**Bret Victor** is arguably the single most influential figure in the modern tools-for-thought space — not because he shipped a product used by millions, but because his talks and essays articulated what computing *should* be with such clarity that they launched entire product categories.

After earning degrees from Caltech and Berkeley, Victor worked at Apple (2007–2011) designing early iPad/iPhone interfaces, prototyping over 70 concept projects. His 2012 talk **"Inventing on Principle"** — viewed over a million times — declared his guiding principle: "Creators need an immediate connection to what they're creating." He demonstrated live-coding where changes instantly appeared in the output, directly inspiring Figma, Observable, Light Table, React Hot Loader, and countless live-coding environments.

His subsequent works built a philosophical architecture for a new computing paradigm. "Magic Ink" (2006) argued most software is information software and should be designed like graphic design. "Explorable Explanations" (2011) coined the term and created the Tangle library for reactive documents. "Up and Down the Ladder of Abstraction" (2011) demonstrated how interactive diagrams enable thinking at multiple scales simultaneously. "Learnable Programming" (2012) critiqued programming environments for failing to make programs visible and tangible. "Seeing Spaces" (2014) proposed rooms filled with visualization tools that make invisible systems visible. And "The Humane Representation of Thought" (2014) — his most philosophical talk — argued that print constrains intellectual capabilities to reading symbols with our eyes, and that a **"dynamic medium"** engaging spatial reasoning, tactile understanding, and kinesthetic intelligence could enable entirely new forms of thought.

These ideas crystallized into **Dynamicland**, a nonprofit research lab founded in 2017 in Oakland. Dynamicland is a communal computer that is a physical place: programs live on printed paper with fiducial markers, ceiling cameras read them, and projectors display output onto surfaces. There are no personal devices. The underlying system, **Realtalk**, is fully self-hosted — the entire OS, programming environment, and tools are built in Realtalk itself. Version control is physical: each version is a different sheet of paper.

The core founding team — Victor, **Josh Horowitz**, **Luke Iannini**, **Toby Schachman**, **Paula Te**, and **Virginia McArthur** — built the system from 2017 onward. Through community hours and residencies, roughly 1,000 participants created hundreds of projects before COVID closed the space in March 2020. The team has since pivoted toward **communal science**, collaborating with UCSF bioscientist Shawn Douglas on DNA origami and nanostructure design using Realtalk. In 2024, they received an **$850,000 Sloan Foundation grant** and are setting up a new space in Berkeley.

The Dynamicland community has been extraordinarily generative. **Toby Schachman** built Shadershop, Apparatus (a hybrid graphics editor and programming environment), and now runs Cuttle, a productionized CAD tool for digital fabrication. **Chaim Gingold**, handpicked by Will Wright for the original Spore team, created Earth: A Primer — an interactive iPad geology book born from Victor's suggestion — and published *Building SimCity* through MIT Press. **Omar Rizwan** created TabFS (mounting browser tabs as a filesystem), Screenotate (OCR for screenshots), and now co-leads **Folk Computer** with Andrés Cuervo — an open-source physical computing system explicitly inspired by Dynamicland. **Glen Chiacchieri** created interactive explorations of mathematics, sorting algorithms, and civil rights documentaries before leaving tech to become a psychotherapist. **Robert Ochshorn** co-founded Reduct.Video, a transcript-based video editing platform built on his open-source forced-alignment tool Gentle.

Victor's influence radiates far beyond direct collaborators. His talks directly inspired **Chris Granger's** Light Table IDE, **Mike Bostock's** Observable notebooks, and design tools like Figma and Webflow. The **Explorable Explanations** movement — now a thriving community with the explorabl.es hub — grew from a 2014 CDG workshop where Vi Hart and **Nicky Case** met and created **Parable of the Polygons**, a playable post on segregation dynamics. Notable Dynamicland volunteers include Andy Matuschak, Kate Compton, Ricardo Cabello (creator of Three.js), and Ivan Zhao (Notion's founder, listed in Dynamicland's Special Thanks).

What remains largely unrealized is Victor's most ambitious vision: communal computing as cultural infrastructure, where computational literacy diffuses throughout society rather than remaining in the hands of specialists. Dynamicland remains small and experimental. But its ideas have seeded dozens of projects, tools, and research programs worldwide.

## Ink & Switch built the infrastructure for local-first spatial tools

**Ink & Switch** is an independent industrial research lab — modeled after Bell Labs and Xerox PARC — that has spent a decade exploring the future of creative tools, local-first software, and spatial thinking interfaces. Founded around 2015 by **Adam Wiggins**, **James Lindenbaum**, and **Orion Henry** (all three co-founders of Heroku), the lab sits between academic research and commercial startups, pursuing "breakthrough ideas with commercial applications on a five- to ten-year time horizon."

The lab's most impactful contribution is the concept of **local-first software**. Their 2019 paper "Local-first software: You own your data, in spite of the cloud" — authored by **Martin Kleppmann**, Wiggins, **Peter van Hardenberg**, and **Mark McGranaghan** — essentially coined the term and launched an entire movement. It articulated seven ideals for software combining cloud collaboration benefits with local data ownership and offline capability, and spawned the Local-First Conference, an active community, and influenced products like Linear and Figma.

The technical backbone is **Automerge**, a CRDT (Conflict-free Replicated Data Type) library enabling collaborative local-first applications. Built through collaboration with Cambridge distributed systems researcher Martin Kleppmann (author of *Designing Data-Intensive Applications*), Automerge allows documents to be modified concurrently and merged automatically. **Automerge 3.0** launched in 2025 with full rich-text support implementing **Peritext** — a CRDT for collaborative rich-text editing co-developed with Notion (whose CEO Ivan Zhao called the collaboration "essential").

On the spatial tools front, Ink & Switch produced **Muse** — a canvas-based spatial thinking tool for iPad that combined freeform spatial organization with deep thinking workflows, handwriting, nested boards, and local-first sync. Spun out as a company in 2019, Muse peaked at ~$120K ARR but never achieved sustainable growth; Adam Wiggins and most of the team departed in fall 2023, leaving it as a solo indie project under Adam Wulf. The research that preceded Muse — particularly **Capstone** (2018, "cards and inking on a freeform canvas") and the **Programmable Ink** thread led by **Szymon Kaliski** — explored how hand-drawn sketches could become programmable like spreadsheets.

**Geoffrey Litt**, now at Notion, has been the lab's leading voice on **malleable software** — the vision that people should be able to reshape tools to their needs with minimal friction. His projects Potluck (dynamic documents from freeform text), Wildcard (browser extension for customizing websites), and Embark (dynamic travel planning documents, with **Alexander Obenauer**) explored end-user programming. The 2025 essay "Malleable Software: Restoring User Agency in a World of Locked-Down Apps" is the lab's most recent manifesto. Other key researchers include **Brooklyn Zelenka** (leading Keyhive for local-first access control), **Ivan Reese** and **Marcel Goethals** (Crosscut, a stylus tool for drawing dynamic models), and **Orion Reed** (programmable canvas tools joining in 2025). Across roughly **39 projects** over its ten-year life, the lab has consistently advanced the idea that spatial, local-first, user-owned tools can serve as genuine thinking environments.

## From Pad++ to infinite canvas: how zooming became a paradigm

The zoomable user interface originated in academia and became one of the defining interaction patterns for spatial tools. In 1993, **Ken Perlin** and David Fox published the foundational "Pad: An Alternative Approach to the Computer Interface" at SIGGRAPH, presenting an infinite two-dimensional information plane navigated by zooming and panning. **Ben Bederson** and James D. Hollan then built **Pad++** at Bellcore (1994), introducing semantic zooming — where objects change their representation based on zoom level rather than simply scaling. With **George Furnas** (who had introduced generalized fisheye views in 1986, proposing the Degree of Interest function that spawned an entire research genre), Bederson developed Space-Scale Diagrams to theorize multiscale interfaces. Bederson later created Jazz (2000) and Piccolo as ZUI toolkits at the University of Maryland.

**Jef Raskin** (1943–2005), creator of the Macintosh project, proposed the most radical ZUI vision in *The Humane Interface* (2000): **ZoomWorld**, where the entire operating system is a single infinite zoomable surface containing all documents. He worked on Archy/THE to implement this, but died before completing the ZUI component.

The term "infinite canvas" was coined by **Scott McCloud** in 1995, originally for webcomics — treating the screen as a window rather than a page. The concept migrated to software design, and today the infinite canvas is the default interface pattern for visual collaboration, brainstorming, and spatial note-taking. The collaborative whiteboard market is projected to reach **$8.11 billion by 2033**.

Modern infinite canvas tools and their designers form a rich ecosystem:

- **Figma** (founded 2012 by **Dylan Field** and **Evan Wallace**): Browser-based design tool whose real-time multiplayer infinite canvas revolutionized design collaboration. Adobe's proposed $20B acquisition was abandoned in 2023 due to regulatory concerns.
- **tldraw** (**Steve Ruiz**): Open-source infinite canvas SDK for the web, both a free whiteboard app and a developer toolkit. His viral "Make Real" experiment (November 2023) — draw a wireframe, AI creates a working UI — is often cited as the first "vibe coding" tool.
- **Miro** (founded 2011 by **Andrey Khusid** and **Oleg Shardin** as RealtimeBoard): Grew from a design agency side project to **100 million users** at a $17.5B valuation, accelerated by pandemic remote work.
- **Prezi** (founded 2009 by architect **Adam Somlai-Fischer**, **Péter Halácsy**, and **Péter Árvai** in Budapest): The first widely popular zooming presentation tool, directly based on ZUI research. Demonstrated mass-market viability of zoomable interfaces.
- **Kinopio** (**Pirijan Ketheswaran**, co-creator of Glitch): A personal spatial thinking tool blending HyperCard aesthetics with Web 1.0 warmth and Japanese game design sensibility.
- **Kosmik** (founded 2018 by **Paul Rony** and **Christophe Van Deputte** in Paris): A spatial browser/canvas built on IPFS, raised $3.7M seed in 2023.
- **Heptabase** (founded 2021 by **Alan Chan**): Visual-first note-taking with spatial canvas, bootstrapped to **$200K/month** revenue, backed by Y Combinator and Kleiner Perkins.
- **Excalidraw** (**Christopher Chedeau** / Vjeux, a Meta engineer who also co-created React Native): Open-source sketchy whiteboard that went viral during the pandemic.

**John Palmer's** essays "Spatial Interfaces" (2019) and "Spatial Software" (2020) provided the field's shared vocabulary. He argued spatial software offers three advantages: **afforded intuition** (naturally understandable), **expressiveness** (more freedom), and **presence** (sensing other users) — and that COVID-19 accelerated adoption as people sought spatial connection in digital spaces.

## Information visualization's deep research foundations

Academic visualization research has built the theoretical and practical infrastructure that spatial tools for thought depend on. The lineage begins with foundational theorists and extends through some of the most influential labs in computing.

**Muriel Cooper** (1925–1994) deserves special recognition as a bridge figure. MIT Press's first design director (she designed its iconic logo), Cooper co-founded the **Visible Language Workshop** at MIT, which became a founding group of the Media Lab in 1985. Her legendary 1994 TED presentation showed student work on **dynamic typography in 3D information spaces** — text floating, stretching, and zooming in "velvety-black computer universe." Nicholas Negroponte called it "the turning point in interface design." Bill Gates personally requested a copy. Cooper died unexpectedly months later; her work anticipated modern spatial interfaces by decades and is now in MoMA's permanent collection. **John Maeda** succeeded her at the Media Lab, and **Lisa Strausfeld** became a partner at Pentagram.

**Hiroshi Ishii**, founding the MIT **Tangible Media Group** in 1995, extended spatial thinking into physical form. His Tangible Bits concept (CHI '97, with Brygg Ullmer) proposed physical embodiment of digital information, and his Radical Atoms vision (2012) imagined hypothetical materials that change form dynamically. Projects like inFORM (a dynamic shape display) demonstrate that information can have physical form.

At the **University of Washington**, **Jeffrey Heer** leads the Interactive Data Lab, which produced one of the most important tool lineages in visualization: Prefuse → Protovis → **D3.js** (2011, with Mike Bostock) → Vega → Vega-Lite. D3 became the IEEE-recognized "de facto standard for web-based interactive visualization." **Arvind Satyanarayan** at MIT, who co-created Vega-Lite with **Dominik Moritz** and Heer, frames his research explicitly as studying **"intelligence augmentation"** — how computational representations amplify cognition.

**Ben Shneiderman** at the University of Maryland pioneered direct manipulation interfaces, invented **treemaps** (1991), created dynamic queries, and articulated the visual information-seeking mantra: **"Overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand."** His collaborator **Tamara Munzner** at UBC authored *Visualization Analysis and Design* (2014), the definitive textbook, and developed the Nested Model for visualization design. At CMU's HCII, **Niki Kittur** studies sensemaking and knowledge work — his stat that "we spend **1 trillion hours a year** making sense of the web" frames the scale of the tools-for-thought challenge.

**Mike Bostock** — Heer's PhD student who created D3.js and later co-founded **Observable** — deserves particular attention. Observable represents the merger of visualization with computational notebooks, descended from the "active essay" tradition. Bostock explicitly cited Bret Victor's critiques of "blindly manipulating symbols" as inspiration.

**Fernanda Viégas** and **Martin Wattenberg**, now both Gordon McKay Professors at Harvard and principal scientists at Google (where they co-founded **PAIR**, People + AI Research), created ManyEyes (the first system for non-experts to create interactive visualizations), Wind Map (in MoMA's permanent collection), and tools for ML interpretability. Their work bridges artistic visualization with AI transparency.

The theoretical genealogy runs from **Jacques Bertin** (whose 1967 *Semiology of Graphics* identified the fundamental visual variables: position, size, shape, orientation, color, value, and texture) through **Leland Wilkinson** (whose 1999 *The Grammar of Graphics* provided a formal compositional system) to **Hadley Wickham** (whose ggplot2 implemented Wilkinson's grammar in R) and the Vega/Vega-Lite ecosystem. Nearly every current visualization tool traces its intellectual DNA to this chain.

## Game designers pioneered spatial knowledge through play

Game designers approach spatial thinking from a fundamentally different angle: they build worlds designed to teach through exploration, not instruction.

**Jonathan Blow's** The Witness (2016) is perhaps the single best example of what tools-for-thought advocates aspire to. An open-world puzzle game on an uninhabited island, it uses the environment itself — architecture, light, shadows, landscape — as the teaching mechanism. Blow never explains the rules; players discover them through interaction. Devon Zuegel called it **"probably the best example of constructivist learning I've ever seen"** and "more impactful than most software that self-identifies as a tool for thought." Blow worked with an architect and landscaper on the game, describing himself as "a more spatial creature." His upcoming Order of the Sinking Star (2026) will be the largest single-player puzzle game ever made, built in his custom language Jai.

**Nicky Case** (b. 1994) is the most prolific creator of explorable explanations. Their works — Parable of the Polygons (with Vi Hart), The Evolution of Trust (game theory), **Loopy** (a systems thinking tool adopted by MITRE), Adventures with Anxiety, How to Remember Anything Forever-ish — demonstrate how complex systems become understandable through play. Case was invited to a Bret Victor workshop after early work went viral, connecting them to the broader Dynamicland community.

**Vi Hart** (b. 1988) made mathematical visual thinking viral through her "Doodling in Math Class" YouTube series, fundamentally demonstrating that mathematical reasoning can be spatial and embodied rather than purely symbolic. As "Resident Mathemusician" at Khan Academy, she co-founded eleVR for VR research and created Hypernom — a game letting players experience 4D polytopes stereographically projected into 3D. She was a Principal Investigator at CDG/HARC alongside Victor and Dan Ingalls.

**Will Wright** articulated the concept of **"possibility spaces"** — game systems generating emergent complexity through interacting rules. SimCity (1989) taught systems thinking to an entire generation; city planners have credited it with inspiring their careers. **Ian Bogost** formalized this with "procedural rhetoric" — the idea that games make arguments through rule-based representations, not through words or images. **Jenova Chen** applied Csíkszentmihályi's flow theory to spatial game design; Journey (2012) achieved wordless narrative through pure spatial traversal. **Jason Rohrer's** Passage (2007) — a 5-minute game simulating an entire human lifespan through spatial mechanics — is in MoMA's permanent video game collection.

The creative coding community provides the technical substrate for spatial experimentation. **Casey Reas** and **Ben Fry** created **Processing** (2001) at the MIT Media Lab — the programming language making visual coding accessible to artists and designers (Golden Nica at Prix Ars Electronica, 2005). **Lauren Lee McCarthy** reimagined it as **p5.js** for the web, now used by over **10 million people**. **Daniel Shiffman's** The Coding Train and *The Nature of Code* teach computational thinking through spatial metaphors — particles, flocking, genetic algorithms. **Zach Lieberman** co-created openFrameworks and co-founded the **School for Poetic Computation** (SFPC, 2013) with Taeyoon Choi, Jen Lowe, and Amit Pitaru — treating code as a medium for poetry and expression, not just engineering.

## Today's tool builders are testing what works in practice

The 2015–2026 era has seen an explosion of practical tool-building, testing which ideas from the theoretical tradition can actually serve daily knowledge work.

**Andy Matuschak** and **Michael Nielsen** anchored the modern movement with their 2019 essay "How can we develop transformative tools for thought?" and the prototype **Quantum Country** — an interactive quantum computing essay with embedded spaced-repetition prompts. Matuschak's concept of **evergreen notes** (atomic, concept-oriented, densely linked) and his Orbit spaced-repetition platform represent the most rigorous experimental approach to augmenting memory and understanding. Nielsen, now at the Astera Institute, has expanded into metascience — redesigning the social infrastructure of scientific discovery.

**Conor White-Sullivan** triggered the "networked thought" wave with **Roam Research** (2017), popularizing bidirectional linking, block references, and the outliner+graph hybrid. The "Roam cult" of 2020–2021 spawned an entire tool category. Roam maintains ~1 million monthly visitors but has lost momentum to faster-moving competitors. **Obsidian** (**Shida Li** and **Erica Xu**, founded 2020) proved the power of the local-first model: **7 full-time staff, zero external funding, $25M ARR, valued at $350M**, with 1.5 million monthly active users. **LogSeq** (**Tienson Qin**, 2020) pursues a similar vision as open source, backed by Patrick Collison and Nat Friedman.

**Gordon Brander** attempted the most architecturally ambitious project with **Subconscious** and the **Noosphere protocol** — a decentralized worldwide thought graph over IPFS. The project wound down in May 2024 after failing to find product-market fit. Brander's candid postmortem acknowledged: "Today, if you want to amplify intelligence, you probably wouldn't build a decentralized notes graph — you would go to work on personal AI."

**Maggie Appleton** has been the field's most thoughtful cultural critic, arguing in "Tools for Thought as Cultural Practices, Not Computational Objects" that these tools must be understood anthropologically, not just technically. Her "Brief History & Ethos of the Digital Garden" (2020) became the definitive account of the digital garden movement. She now designs AI research interfaces at scientific startups. **Alexander Obenauer** pursues the most radical OS-level rethinking with his **Itemized OS** concept and WonderOS experiments — treating every digital thing as an "item" in one unified graph. **Linus Lee** built Monocle (universal personal search across all personal data) and now works at the intersection of AI and creative tools at Thrive Capital.

The major product teams reflect distinct philosophies: **Notion** (Ivan Zhao's "Lego for productivity" — composable blocks, now integrating AI deeply), **Coda** (Shishir Mehrotra's "docs as apps"), **Tana** (Stian Håklev's supertags for structured data), and **Heptabase** (Alan Chan's "Notion and Figma had a baby" — spatial canvas for deep learning). **Fermat** offers AI-powered collaborative whiteboards for creative teams.

## The algorithmic foundations of spatial layout

Every node-link diagram, treemap, and graph visualization depends on layout algorithms developed over four decades, each solving a fundamental problem: how to arrange information in space to reveal structure.

**Force-directed graph layouts** began with **Peter Eades'** 1984 spring embedder (vertices repel, edges attract), refined by **Kamada and Kawai** (1989, using graph-theoretic distances) and **Fruchterman and Reingold** (1991, adding temperature-based convergence). The practical lineage runs through **d3-force** (Bostock's widely-used web implementation) to modern multilevel approaches. **Stephen Kobourov** at Arizona authored the definitive handbook chapter on these algorithms.

Shneiderman's **treemaps** (1991) were refined by **Mark Bruls, Kees Huizing, and Jarke van Wijk** into squarified treemaps (2000) at Eindhoven, by van Wijk into cushion treemaps (1999), and by **Martin Wattenberg** into the SmartMoney "Map of the Market" — the first web treemap. **Michael Balzer and Oliver Deussen** developed Voronoi treemaps, enabling treemaps within arbitrary shapes.

**Hyperbolic trees** emerged from Xerox PARC, where **John Lamping, Ramana Rao, and Peter Pirolli** (1995) laid hierarchy uniformly on a hyperbolic plane and mapped it via the Poincaré disk model. The technique is experiencing a renaissance through **Poincaré embeddings** (Nickel & Kiela, 2017) for representing hierarchical data in machine learning.

**Dimensionality reduction** — crucial for visualizing knowledge spaces — progressed from **t-SNE** (Laurens van der Maaten and Geoffrey Hinton, 2008, 30,000+ citations) to **UMAP** (Leland McInnes, 2018, based on Riemannian geometry), which preserves global structure better. Both are now standard tools for creating "maps" of document embeddings and semantic spaces.

Other essential inventions include **Alfred Inselberg's** parallel coordinates (1985), **Kozo Sugiyama's** layered graph drawing framework (1981, foundational to GraphViz), and the **focus+context** family — perspective walls, cone trees (Robertson, Mackinlay, Card at PARC, 1991), the table lens (Rao and Card, 1994), and the comprehensive framework provided by Furnas's original fisheye work. Recent frontiers include LLM-assisted layout generation and graph foundation models.

## Embodied cognition explains why spatial tools work

The theoretical justification for spatial tools for thought comes from cognitive science research demonstrating that **human cognition is fundamentally spatial and embodied**.

**George Lakoff** and **Mark Johnson's** *Metaphors We Live By* (1980) showed that abstract thought is structured through spatial bodily experience: "MORE IS UP," "ARGUMENT IS WAR," "TIME IS MOTION." These aren't poetic flourishes — they're the cognitive architecture through which we reason. **Andy Clark** and **David Chalmers'** Extended Mind thesis (1998) argued that when external objects are coupled to the brain in the right way — a notebook reliably storing beliefs, a spatial canvas organizing ideas — they become literal parts of the cognitive system. **Edwin Hutchins'** *Cognition in the Wild* (1995) demonstrated through Navy ship navigation studies that cognitive processes are distributed across people, tools, and spatial arrangements.

**Barbara Tversky** crystallized this most directly for the tools-for-thought community in *Mind in Motion* (2019): **"Spatial thinking, rooted in perception of space and action in it, is the foundation for all thought."** Her research shows we think in space before we think in words, that gesturing shapes the communicator's own thoughts (subjects forced to sit on their hands fell into "fumbling incoherence"), and that sequences of still images often outperform animations because people can move through them at their own pace.

**David Kirsh** and **Paul Maglio's** concept of **epistemic actions** (1994) provides the mechanism: physical manipulations of the environment that simplify problem-solving by reducing memory demands, computation steps, and error probability. Advanced Tetris players physically rotate pieces rather than mentally rotating them — and this extends to how we arrange notes on a desk, organize sticky notes on a wall, or drag cards across an infinite canvas.

Spatial computing platforms are beginning to implement these insights. **Apple Vision Pro** (launched February 2024, updated with M5 chip in 2025) positions apps in physical space around the user. **Mark Weiser's** vision of ubiquitous computing (1991) — "the most profound technologies are those that disappear" — and Dynamicland's room-as-computer paradigm represent opposite poles: computation woven into the environment versus computation projected onto physical surfaces. Research on VR memory palaces (Molloy & Sykes, 2022; Huttner et al., 2019) shows that immersive spatial navigation strengthens encoding and recall, connecting ancient mnemonic techniques to digital spatial tools.

## Digital gardens, wikis, and the knowledge graph community

The digital garden movement reconnects modern tools to the original hypertext vision. **Mike Caulfield's** 2015 essay "The Garden and the Stream" articulated the foundational distinction: the web evolved from a garden (topological, spatial, integrative, accumulating knowledge) into a stream (chronological, timeline-based, individualistic). He identified Bush's Memex as "the original garden."

**Ward Cunningham** invented the wiki in 1995 (WikiWikiWeb) and later created **Federated Wiki** (2011), adding Git-like forking so users maintain their own copies of wiki pages — creating "a chorus of voices" rather than forced consensus. **Jeremy Ruston's** TiddlyWiki (2004) — a non-linear personal notebook contained entirely in a single HTML file with no external dependencies — has maintained a devoted community for 20+ years. **Gyuri Lajos** pursues the most Nelson-faithful approach with WikiNizer, TrailMarks, and IndyHub — interpersonal knowledge graph tools built on IPFS for decentralized peer-to-peer knowledge networking.

On the academic side, **Joel Chan** at UMD studies **discourse graphs** — knowledge structures composed of granular units (theories, claims, evidence) and rhetorical relationships. His argument that the narrative document is the wrong primary unit for scholarly knowledge synthesis drives work with **Matt Akamatsu** on infrastructure for collective synthesis. **Athens Research** (Y Combinator W21), an open-source Roam alternative founded by Jeff Tang, was discontinued in February 2023.

The **Zettelkasten** tradition — originating with German sociologist **Niklas Luhmann**, who used ~90,000 index cards to write 70 books and 400+ articles — was popularized by **Sönke Ahrens'** *How to Take Smart Notes* (2017), which transformed it from academic legend to learnable method. **Christian Tietze's** Zettelkasten.de community serves as the intellectual center for practitioners. **Tiago Forte's** Building a Second Brain methodology represents a more productivity-oriented cousin, using the PARA framework (Projects, Areas, Resources, Archives). **Chris Aldrich** bridges the historical tradition of commonplace books with the IndieWeb and Hypothes.is communities, and **Nikita Voloboev's** "Everything I Know" wiki pioneered the practice of publishing one's entire knowledge base publicly.

## Design studios and information designers shape visual culture

The visual language of spatial tools for thought owes much to practitioners working at the intersection of design, data, and narrative.

**Giorgia Lupi** is perhaps the most important figure connecting data visualization to humanistic values. Her **Data Humanism** philosophy — challenging the impersonality of data and designing visual narratives that reconnect numbers to human stories — culminated in her becoming the first data visualization practitioner to join **Pentagram** as a partner (2019) and the first to receive the **Cooper-Hewitt National Design Award** for Communication Design (2022). Her year-long **Dear Data** collaboration with **Stefanie Posavec** — weekly hand-drawn data postcards sent transatlantically — was acquired by MoMA's permanent collection in 2016.

**The Pudding** (founded 2017 by **Matt Daniels** and **Russell Goldenberg**) represents a new model: a collective of ~6–8 "journalist-engineers" creating visual essays explaining cultural ideas, each person handling research, data analysis, design, writing, and code. **Stamen Design** (founded 2001 by **Eric Rodenbeck**) won a 2017 National Design Award for work including open map tiles used on thousands of sites and the Atlas of Emotions for the Dalai Lama. **Fathom Information Design** (founded 2010 by **Ben Fry**, co-creator of Processing) builds "platforms, tools, and products for understanding data" — emphasizing helping clients think about their information.

The historical lineage runs deeper than computing. **Richard Saul Wurman** coined "Information Architect" and founded TED (1984), developing the LATCH principles — Location, Alphabet, Time, Category, Hierarchy — as the only five ways to organize information. **Buckminster Fuller's** Dymaxion map challenged conventional projections, and his whole-systems thinking influenced **Stewart Brand's** Whole Earth Catalog, connecting counterculture to computing. **Charles and Ray Eames'** *Powers of Ten* (1977) demonstrated multiscale thinking — the conceptual ancestor of zoomable interfaces. **Otto Neurath's** ISOTYPE system (1920s) pioneered pictographic communication for democracy, inventing the role of the "Transformer" who translates complex data into clear visual forms — anticipating modern data journalism. **Jacques Bertin's** *Semiology of Graphics* (1967) identified the fundamental visual variables that remain the foundation of visualization theory. And **William Playfair** (1759–1823) invented the line chart, bar chart, and pie chart — the Big Bang of statistical graphics that **Edward Tufte** celebrates as the beginning of the entire tradition.

## The invisible threads connecting everything

The most striking feature of this field is how densely interconnected it is. A few key threads weave through every section:

**The augmentation thread** runs from Bush's Memex through Licklider's "symbiosis," Engelbart's "augmenting human intellect," Kay's "metamedium," Victor's "humane representation of thought," to Matuschak and Nielsen's "transformative tools for thought." Each generation reframes the same conviction: that external representations fundamentally shape what humans can think.

**The spatial cognition thread** connects Lakoff and Johnson's conceptual metaphors to Tversky's "mind in motion," Clark's extended mind, Hutchins' distributed cognition, Kirsh's epistemic actions, and the practical implementations in zoomable interfaces, infinite canvases, and tangible interfaces. The empirical finding is clear: **spatial organization isn't just a visual convenience — it aligns with cognitive architecture**.

**The constructionist thread** runs from Papert's "objects to think with" through Kay's Smalltalk and HyperCard, to Blow's constructivist puzzle design, Case's explorable explanations, Gingold's interactive science books, the creative coding community's treatment of code as thinking material, and the malleable software vision of Ink & Switch. The shared principle: people learn by building and manipulating external artifacts.

**The democratization thread** connects HyperCard (anyone can build interactive media), Processing/p5.js (anyone can create visual code), wikis (anyone can edit knowledge), digital gardens (anyone can publish evolving ideas), and Dynamicland (everyone is an author, not a user). The persistent tension: tools that are powerful enough to augment expert thinking versus tools accessible enough for universal adoption.

**The institutional thread** shows how a small number of places — ARPA/IPTO, Xerox PARC, MIT Media Lab, UMD HCIL, Stanford HCI, CMU HCII, UW IDL, Ink & Switch, Dynamicland — have generated a disproportionate share of the ideas and people driving this field. And the funding thread reveals how patrons like Licklider, Patrick Collison (who backed both LogSeq and Dynamicland), Sam Altman (who briefly funded HARC before redirecting enthusiasm to OpenAI), and the Sloan Foundation have shaped which visions get pursued.

The field's biggest open question in 2026 is whether AI changes everything or nothing. Gordon Brander's shutdown of Subconscious — concluding that "if you want to amplify intelligence, you would go to work on personal AI" — represents one pole. The other pole is represented by Litt's malleable software research, Victor's communal computing, and Obsidian's remarkable success proving that thoughtfully designed, local-first, human-directed tools can thrive even in the AI era. The tools-for-thought tradition suggests the answer is neither extreme: AI is a new material for building thinking tools, but the design principles — spatial organization, immediate feedback, user agency, external representation — remain as fundamental as ever. The architects of spatial thought have been building toward this moment for 80 years.
